# Scene Writer Distillation — Writers Guild Foundation V0.1

## A. Source Scope

**Actually read:** four public WGF Screenplay Primers: *Dialogue*, *Subtext*, *Character Voice*, and *Backstory and Exposition*. The reviewed body sections include each article’s central explanation and its final diagnostic questions/exercise.

**Not read or transferred:** the WGF Primer index as evidence, full copyrighted scripts used in the primers’ examples, any named writer’s dialogue style, Showrunner’s macro-story authority, QA’s language-correction authority, or any rule about production cost, AI production, directing, acting, art, or continuity.

**Evidence boundary:** WGF directly supports dialogue friction, conflicting objectives, subtext techniques, contextual factors that influence speech, and clue-based character exposition. It does not directly establish a scene turn, end point, entry/exit convention, or AI-production constraint.

## B. Primary Contributions

- Dialogue becomes dramatically active when scene partners’ objectives, philosophies, or approaches are at odds.
- Subtext can be built by a discrepancy between the literal topic/utterance and the character’s protected concern; it is a repertoire, not a command to obscure every statement.
- A character’s speech is shaped by social context, occupation/education, self-view, immediate emotion, scene objective, and relationship to the other character.
- Character exposition can be delivered as relevant clues that create intrigue; “show” does not mean revealing everything at once.

## C. Distilled Methods

### SW-D09 — Dialogue Friction Check

- **Capability Name:** Dialogue Friction Check
- **Problem It Solves:** Dialogue may sound fluent but leaves two characters with no competing will, pressure, or tactical consequence.
- **Trigger / Detection:** A scene has conversation, but no answer to what each participant wants or how their aims conflict.
- **Decision Principle:** Test the scene type and each character’s objective/philosophy against the partner’s; friction can arise from opposing approaches, not only overt argument.
- **Action / Transformation:** State each participant’s current aim and the point of opposition; revise only the dialogue/action that fails to enact that opposition.
- **Stop Condition:** Stop when the aim and resistance are playable; do not force a fight, seduction, or negotiation label onto every scene.
- **Failure Risk:** Confusing interpersonal disagreement with conflict in all scenes, or writing dialogue solely to sound sharp.
- **Classification:** `Default Heuristic`.
- **Source Trace:** E-WGF-01, “WRITING YOUR SCREENPLAY: DIALOGUE”, central friction discussion and final questions on scene type, character objectives/philosophies, and their opposition.

### SW-D10 — Subtext-by-Displacement Option

- **Capability Name:** Subtext-by-Displacement Option
- **Problem It Solves:** A scene needs a character to protect, test, disguise, or indirectly expose a concern, but the draft either states it flatly or replaces it with unexplained vagueness.
- **Trigger / Detection:** The approved scene intent calls for a gap between literal language and an actionable relational concern.
- **Decision Principle:** Subtext may use a stand-in subject, an apparently small dispute, or an inverse utterance when the surrounding action makes the underlying pressure inferable.
- **Action / Transformation:** Choose one indirect carrier only if it advances the active scene relation; retain sufficient behavior and context for an audience to infer rather than decode a puzzle.
- **Stop Condition:** Stop when the hidden concern has a playable effect; do not explain it in a narrator voice.
- **Failure Risk:** Treating all direct dialogue as bad, or creating “mystery” with no observable stakes.
- **Classification:** `Optional Tool`.
- **Source Trace:** E-WGF-02, “WRITING YOUR SCREENPLAY: SUBTEXT”, closing exercise offers third-person/stand-in stories, trivial disputes, and inverse compliments as ways to practice subtext.

### SW-D11 — Clue-Scaled Exposition

- **Capability Name:** Clue-Scaled Exposition
- **Problem It Solves:** Backstory/world information is either dumped all at once or withheld until the audience cannot form a working inference.
- **Trigger / Detection:** The scene gives total explanation without current dramatic relevance, or removes so much context that the audience cannot follow the character’s situation.
- **Decision Principle:** Treat exposition as character revelation through clues, hints, and relevant detail; “show me” is not “show me everything.”
- **Action / Transformation:** Select the smallest detail that changes how the audience reads the current character/action; defer only the information whose absence does not break the scene’s needed comprehension.
- **Stop Condition:** Stop when the audience has a usable inference and the scene’s current action remains primary.
- **Failure Risk:** Equating intrigue with confusion or treating every disclosed fact as a dump.
- **Classification:** `Default Heuristic`.
- **Source Trace:** E-WGF-04, “WRITING YOUR SCREENPLAY: BACKSTORY AND EXPOSITION”, discussion of clue/nugget/hint-based character revelation and its closing character-detail exercise.

### SW-D12 — Contextual Speech-Pressure Map

- **Capability Name:** Contextual Speech-Pressure Map
- **Problem It Solves:** Dialogue is drafted without the scene-specific conditions that make a character’s choice of words and silence meaningful.
- **Trigger / Detection:** All characters respond from a generic voice, or a line has no relation to the speaker’s immediate objective, self-view, social context, or relationship.
- **Decision Principle:** Before drafting an important exchange, map the relevant contextual factors: community/origin, education or occupation, self-view, leading emotion, scene objective, and relationship.
- **Action / Transformation:** Use the map to choose what the character attempts, avoids, concedes, or risks in the scene. It may inform wording, but it does not authorize lexical correction or a style score.
- **Stop Condition:** Stop after the scene pressure is defined; route language-quality and voice-preservation judgments to Shared QA.
- **Failure Risk:** Stereotyping speech from identity markers or turning contextual notes into a fixed character voice imitation.
- **Classification:** `Conditional Method` — when drafting or revising character interaction in a specific scene.
- **Source Trace:** E-WGF-03, “WRITING YOUR SCREENPLAY: CHARACTER VOICE”, factors influencing speech include context, occupation/education, self-view, emotion, scene objective, and relationship.

### SW-D13 — Leave Performable Space

- **Capability Name:** Leave Performable Space
- **Problem It Solves:** Scene writing tries to guarantee distinct voice by over-prescribing accents, stammers, cadence, or performance, leaving no room for actors and filmmakers.
- **Trigger / Detection:** The draft relies on heavy phonetic/performative scripting rather than conflict context and objective.
- **Decision Principle:** Character distinctness needs balance: enough differentiation to make interaction readable, but not so much that the page becomes labored or pre-empts collaborators.
- **Action / Transformation:** Reduce nonessential performance specification; retain the contextual pressure and active difference between characters.
- **Stop Condition:** Stop when the scene supplies playable intentions and relationships, not an actor-execution blueprint.
- **Failure Risk:** Flattening a canonically important speech pattern or incorrectly treating Scene Writer as final voice QA.
- **Classification:** `Hard Constraint` for Scene Writer role boundaries.
- **Source Trace:** E-WGF-03, “WRITING YOUR SCREENPLAY: CHARACTER VOICE”, discussion of balance and leaving room for actors and filmmakers.

## E. Studio Translation

WGF becomes Scene Writer scene-construction capability in three narrow forms: give dialogue conflicting immediate aims, use subtext only when its relational pressure remains playable, and release information as action-relevant clues. Character-voice material is translated into an interaction-context map, not a language-cleanup or personal-style simulator. Shared QA remains responsible for checking language and voice preservation after scene material exists.

## F. Non-Transferable Content

- Specific copyrighted screenplay examples, lines, characters, and named writers are not retained or emulated.
- The “fight/seduction/negotiation” framing is a diagnostic prompt, not a universal scene taxonomy.
- Individual accents, stammers, and speech quirks are not a reusable Studio voice template.
- WGF’s methods do not grant Showrunner authority, Directing, Acting, Art, Continuity, cost, or AI-production authority to Scene Writer.
- No direct source support exists for a scene turn, scene end point, entry/exit rule, production cost, or AI production.
